Context: Thumb Party is a family party game on a TV (or one phone or tablet hosting) with phones as controllers, for groups that include children around 8. 1 to 4 players. Controls per player: a D-pad or stick (or tilt) to move, A, B, X, Y, a right stick on some phones; phones talk to the host over Wi-Fi, so expect 30–80 ms of lag and the occasional hiccup. Every game draws from a state snapshot the host sends to the phones. Our own characters and art only (Google Play Families policy: no violence, nothing scary); describe other games in your own words.

The Arena today: a v1 test game. Each player is a marble with eyes. Everyone starts on one side and races through walls and moving hazards (sliding or circling) to a goal; A dashes, with a cooldown; hitting a wall or hazard buzzes the phone; the first to the goal wins the round, then the field mirrors and the next round starts. Players pass through each other: nothing a player does touches another player. Shape Race (the puzzle game, two sides) is getting M14's party night: points, 3 or 5 rounds with a scoreboard, the cat (a warned chaos event), and end-of-night awards.

Questions (answer each with the URL of the page that says so, and its date):
1. Party minigames of the Arena's kind (top-down or small-arena, move and one or two buttons, 2–4 players at once): in Mario Party (Superstars, Super Mario Party, Jamboree), LEGO Party (2025), Wii Party, Pummel Party, Fall Guys, Party Animals, Ultimate Chicken Horse, Bomberman, Overcooked, Moving Out, and AirConsole's phone-controller games (the closest to us). Name 10–15 concrete game types (tag, king of the hill, coin grab, bumper marbles, hot potato, last one standing, territory painting, relay, co-op carry, ...): how each plays, round length, how it ends and scores.
2. What players and reviewers find fun and not fun in these (Nintendo Life, IGN, Eurogamer, Polygon, GameSpot, Common Sense Media, Game Developer, player discussions): which types are remembered as the best, which as boring, luck-heavy, confusing or unfair, and why. What makes players laugh together; what makes children around 8 cry or quit (being knocked out early and waiting, being ganged up on, not knowing who they are).
3. Players touching each other: bumping, pushing, stealing, tagging. What makes it fun and fair (weight, knockback limits, invincibility after a hit, respawn quickly), and how games keep an eliminated player busy (ghosts, a spectator role, quick respawn). Anything on doing this with lag over Wi-Fi or with phone controllers (AirConsole developer docs or talks, Jackbox, couch games streamed).
4. Teams and modes: free-for-all, 2 v 2, 1 v 3 (Mario Party's), co-op against the game. Which work for mixed ages (a parent and a child), and how games balance a strong player against a weak one without it feeling like charity.
5. Party elements around a minigame: a short rule card before it, practice time, a countdown, coins or points earned in it feeding a party total, bonus awards, an item or power-up, a chaos event, a final-round twist. Which make a short round feel like part of a party night.
6. Variety: how games keep one arena fresh across a night (rotating rules on one field, random modifiers, several small maps).

Then propose. **Enhancements for the Arena**, 6–10 of them, ranked, each with: what it is in two or three lines; why it would be fun (cite question 1–6 findings); what can go wrong for an 8-year-old and how to prevent it; how it fits our controls, the lag and a snapshot; what it reuses from today's Arena (movement, dash, walls, hazards, mirror) and what is new; effort (S, M, L); and whether it belongs in M14 (beside Shape Race in the party night: points, rounds, scoreboard, awards) or later (M15 power-ups and more chaos). Include at least: one mode where players touch each other, one team mode, one co-op mode, and how an Arena round earns party-night points the same way Shape Race does. End with your top three and one sentence each on why, and the questions the owner should decide.

In the report: one section per question, facts as short bullets each with its URL and date; a "Not confirmed" list; the ranked proposals; a 15-line summary. Keep it under 260 lines. Prefer dated reviews and articles, official pages and designer talks; wikis are fine for rules, marked as wikis.
